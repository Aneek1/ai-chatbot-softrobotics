# Language identification

Every number here comes from a results file in this repository. The tables in
[training/RESULTS.md](../training/RESULTS.md) are generated by `training/results_table.py`; nothing
is typed by hand. Where a figure is quoted from someone else's paper it is cited.

## The problem

The application answers in the language you write in, so it has to identify that language from a
single short question. Two things make that hard.

**Coverage.** A user may write in a language the application does not support. It still has to
recognise what they wrote rather than guess. GlotLID covers about two thousand languages, which is
what makes that possible — but the published model is **1.69 GB** and loads its matrices into
memory, on a laptop that is also running a local answer model.

**Confusable pairs.** A general detector is weakest exactly where this application is judged: Malay
against Indonesian, Simplified against Traditional Chinese, and romanized Hindi against Urdu and
English. Those pairs share vocabulary or a script, and a short question gives a detector little to
work with.

## Compression: 1.69 GB to 225 MB, at no cost

GlotLID is product-quantized with fastText's own quantization, without vocabulary pruning, by
`scripts/compress_glotlid.py`. Measured on the FLORES-200 devtest set — **206,448 sentences** — in
GlotLID's own label space:

| Model | Figures from | Languages | Macro-F1 | Macro-FPR |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| glotlid-full | this repository | 195 | 0.955 | 0.0002 |
| glotlid-compressed | this repository | 195 | **0.957** | 0.0002 |
| GlotLID-M | Kargaran et al. 2023 ([arXiv:2310.16248](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16248)), Table 2 | 196 | 0.917 | 0.0042 |
| OpenLID | Kargaran et al. 2023, Table 3 | 195 | 0.923 | 0.0051 |
| OpenLID | Burchell et al. 2023 ([arXiv:2305.13820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13820)), Table 1 | 201 | 0.927 | 0.033 |

Two things worth stating plainly. **Compression did not cost accuracy** — 0.955 to 0.957 is within
the noise of this comparison, and the point is that quantizing to 13% of the size did not hurt, not
that it helped. And the reproduction scores **above** the published figure, which usually means the
evaluation differs somewhere rather than that the model is better; the decision record
[0001](decisions/0001-compressed-glotlid.md) records the choice and its consequences.

Burchell et al.'s table does not say whether its FPR is a fraction or a percentage, which is why
that row is quoted rather than compared against.

## The specialist: choosing inside the confusion groups

Stage one is the compressed GlotLID. When its top label falls inside one of the three confusion
groups, stage two asks a specialist trained here to choose within that group, and ignores any label
it returns from outside it. Until a specialist is configured, Han-script text falls back to an
OpenCC conversion rule, reported as stage `rule`. See decision
[0002](decisions/0002-two-stage-detection.md).

Sixteen runs across three architectures were trained and compared. Metric is validation macro-F1
over the confusion-group labels; the full table, including every run, is in
[training/RESULTS.md](../training/RESULTS.md).

| Run | Family | Group macro-F1 | malay/indonesian | han | romanized hindi | Size | Train |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| e5-finetune | fine-tuned encoder | **0.913** | 0.948 | 0.985 | 0.842 | 470.7 MB | 36 min |
| e5-head | classifier on frozen embeddings | 0.881 | 0.905 | 0.979 | 0.799 | **0.8 MB** | 1 min |
| fasttext | fastText classifier | 0.876 | 0.944 | 0.978 | 0.764 | 356.1 MB | 2 min |

The fine-tune was selected and ships as the default.

**The interesting result is the middle row.** A 0.8 MB classifier over frozen multilingual-e5-small
embeddings reaches within **3.2 points** of a fine-tune **560 times its size**, and trains in one
minute rather than thirty-six. If the deployment target were a device rather than a laptop, that is
the row to look at — which is why the next piece of work is measuring what each actually costs to
run, in [coreml-lid-task.md](coreml-lid-task.md).

## Where it is weakest

**Romanized Hindi, at 0.842 even in the best model.** Hindi written in Latin script against Urdu and
English is the hardest of the three groups and the one the numbers are worst on. It is also where
the application's end-to-end answer-language accuracy is lowest: 0.450 for romanized Hindi and 0.700
for Hindi in Devanagari, over the 200-question evaluation. A user asking in either often gets an
answer in another language, and the interface shows the detected language with its raw label so that
this is visible rather than hidden.

Two further limits worth stating:

- **Short input is not separately measured.** Identification from three words behaves differently
  from a paragraph, and the evaluation does not break results down by input length. That gap is
  known and unmeasured, which is a different thing from being small.
- **Code-switching is not handled.** A question mixing two languages gets one label.
